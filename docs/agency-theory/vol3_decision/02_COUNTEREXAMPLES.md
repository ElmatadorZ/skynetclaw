# Agency — Volume III · 02 — Counterexamples & Boundary Cases

> Pure philosophy. Deliverables 5 (boundary cases) + 6 (counterexamples) + Question 7.
> Six canonical cases, each a stress-test of the ontology (Decision = closure +
> commitment, combining belief × value). They probe the boundaries and — crucially —
> three of them (Newcomb, Buridan, Akrasia) directly test the Commitment hypothesis and
> the belief/value inputs.

---

## The cases

| Case | What it stresses | Verdict for the ontology |
|---|---|---|
| **Lottery / coin-flip** | is arbitrary selection a decision? | *deciding to randomize* is a decision; the draw is not — separates the committing act from the chance event |
| **Buridan's ass** | flat value → can a decision form? | commitment sometimes must be *created*, not *derived* — supports Decision ⊋ value read-off |
| **Newcomb** | is decision about *causing* or *evidencing* good outcomes? | routes the belief-input through Part I's causation (do vs see) — the deepest bridge |
| **Prisoner's Dilemma** | is "the rational decision" well-defined among agents? | single-agent decision theory is incomplete for strategic settings — a scope boundary |
| **Akrasia** | can one decide and not act? | yes → Commitment is a real stage distinct from Action (indirect proof) |
| **Bounded rationality** | must a decision optimize? | no → decision is closure+commitment, not optimality (ontology-first vindicated) |

## Dissected

### Lottery / coin-flip — *the commitment/chance separation*
Flipping a coin to choose seems to make the coin decide. It does not: the agent *decides
to defer to the coin* (a real decision — it closes deliberation and commits to abiding by
the result), and the coin then *selects* without deciding. This cleanly separates two
things the word "choice" blurs: the **committing act** (deciding to randomize — has all
of D1–D4) and the **selection event** (the draw — has none). It also shows commitment can
attach to a *procedure* ("I will do whatever the coin says"), not only an option — a
generalization Vol IV (Planning) will need. SUPPORTED.

### Buridan's ass — *commitment created, not derived*
Two equidistant, equal-value bales → EU is silent (a tie), yet a live agent must not
starve. The resolution is a **manufactured commitment**: a resolute tie-break the agent
*creates* rather than reads off value (Chang's parity; resolute choice). This is decisive
for the ontology: **if decisions were mere value read-outs, Buridan would be fatal;
because a decision is a *commitment-forming act*, it can settle what value leaves open.**
The commitment is not justified by the value differential (there is none) but by the
*need to close* — deliberation's cost itself becomes the reason to commit. This previews
the failure axis (file 03): the healthy decision closes at the right time, and sometimes
"right" means "now, arbitrarily," rather than "never, pending a difference that will not
come." SUPPORTED, and a strong plank for the Commitment thesis.

### Newcomb's problem — *the belief-input is a causation question*
One box or two? **Causal Decision Theory** (two-box) says: your choice cannot cause what
is already in the boxes, so take both. **Evidential Decision Theory** (one-box) says:
one-boxing is *evidence* of the predictor's favourable prediction, so one-box. The split
is *exactly* Part I's causation vs correlation: CDT uses **interventionist** causation
(`do(·)`, Pearl — the hinge doc's recovered tool), EDT uses **observational** conditioning
(`see(·)`). **Newcomb is where the Knowing-side causation theory directly fixes the
Acting-side decision:** the belief-input to Decision is not a bare probability but a
*causal* structure, and which causal reading you use *is* the decision theory. This
vindicates the confluence thesis (Vol III·00): the belief that flows into a decision
carries Part I's full causal apparatus, not just a number. **Verdict: the ontology
survives and is enriched** — it does not adjudicate CDT vs EDT (OPEN, file 04) but shows
the dispute *is* a Part-I causation dispute wearing Acting-side clothes. SUPPORTED as the
diagnosis; the resolution OPEN.

### Prisoner's Dilemma — *the strategic boundary*
Each agent's individually-rational decision (defect dominates) yields a collectively
worse outcome than mutual cooperation. This is not a failure of *deciding* — each agent
decides competently — but a demonstration that **"the rational decision" is not
well-defined when outcomes depend on other agents' simultaneous decisions.** Single-agent
decision theory (Vols III) presupposes a *fixed* environment; strategic settings make the
environment itself another decider, and no dominant notion of "the rational choice"
survives (game theory replaces it with equilibrium concepts, themselves plural). **Verdict:
scope boundary — Vol III is single-agent; multi-agent decision is deferred** (it belongs
with Governance, Vol VII, where the interaction of many agents' commitments is the
subject). The ontology holds *within* an agent; it does not claim to close strategic
interaction. Owned, not hidden. SUPPORTED as a boundary.

### Akrasia — *Commitment is a stage, proven by its failure*
The agent decides A is best, forms the intention, and does B. This is *only intelligible*
if the decision produced a **commitment** (to A) that the **action** then failed to
honour (Vol I F5, the Intention→Action severed edge). Were Decision identical to Action,
akrasia would be a contradiction ("did A and not-A"). Its mere *possibility* is a proof
that **Decision produces a distinct, prior commitment** — the exact stage Q10 posits.
Akrasia is thus not a counterexample to the ontology but its strongest confirmation: the
ontology *predicts a gap where akrasia lives*. SUPPORTED (and it independently supports
the Commitment thesis).

### Bounded rationality — *decision is not optimization*
Real agents *satisfice* (Simon): they close on the first option clearing an aspiration
level, under time and compute limits, never surveying the full act×state matrix. If
decision *were* EU-maximization, no bounded agent would ever decide — absurd, since they
decide constantly. Therefore **decision is closure+commitment, not optimality**; EU is an
unreachable *ideal* against which decisions are *graded*, not what a decision *is*. This
retroactively justifies the mission's constraint: had we started from EU, bounded
rationality would look like a *defect*; starting from the ontology, it is simply the
*normal case*, and EU the limiting idealization. SUPPORTED, and it closes the loop with
Q1.

## What the counterexamples establish
1. **Commitment is real and separable** — Buridan (commitment created), Akrasia
   (commitment un-executed), Lottery (commitment to a procedure) all isolate the
   commitment stage from value, action, and selection respectively. Three independent
   confirmations. SUPPORTED.
2. **The belief-input carries Part I whole** — Newcomb shows the probability flowing into
   a decision is really a *causal structure*; the decision inherits the causation theory,
   not a scalar. SUPPORTED.
3. **Decision ≠ optimization; the ontology is framework-neutral** — Bounded rationality
   and Buridan both break EU while leaving *deciding* intact, vindicating recover-first.
   SUPPORTED.
4. **Two boundaries are honestly out of scope** — strategic multi-agent (PD → Vol VII)
   and the CDT/EDT resolution (Newcomb → open). Located, not resolved. SUPPORTED as
   boundaries.

## Falsifiers
The Commitment-as-stage claim fails if akrasia is shown incoherent / eliminable (no gap
between deciding and acting). The confluence claim fails if Newcomb-type cases are shown
to need *no* causal structure in the belief-input (a purely non-causal probability
suffices for all decisions). The "decision ≠ optimization" claim fails if every genuine
decision is shown to be covert optimization (bounded agents included) — satisficing
resists this.
